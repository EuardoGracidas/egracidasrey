About me

Brief profile

Biomedical engineer turned computer scientist, from Puebla to St. John’s.

I am a Biomedical Engineer from Puebla, Mexico, currently pursuing a Master’s degree in Computer Science at Memorial University of Newfoundland. I began my undergraduate studies in Biomedical Engineering at Universidad de las Américas Puebla (UDLAP) in 2022, and from very early on I got involved in research, academic events and student competitions such as EXPOCEM at UDLAP, SILLONDROMO at the Instituto Nacional de Astrofísica, Óptica y Electrónica (INAOE), CNIB 2025 and ICOKG 2025, where my work received several awards and recognitions.

In parallel with my university studies, I completed professional certifications and specialized courses through institutions such as the University of Toronto, IEEE, Nature Masterclasses and Boston Scientific, as well as a Diploma in Neurolearning from Universidad Anáhuac.

After completing my research thesis in 2026, I graduated with honours, receiving the distinction of summa cum laude. During the same period, I published a scientific article in Biology, an MDPI journal (Q1; Impact Factor: 4.3; CiteScore: 7.3), presenting the research I conducted at INAOE during my social service and professional internship. Shortly afterwards, I was awarded a fully funded scholarship to pursue an M.Sc. in Computer Science at Memorial, where I began my graduate studies in September 2026. This transition allows me to bring together biomedical engineering, scientific research and advanced training in computer science.

Temperature-Dependent Tethered Locomotion Behavior in the Madagascar Hissing Cockroach Using a Controlled-Environment Treadmill Platform with Exploratory Illumination Assays

Eduardo Gracidas-Reyes, Gerardo Díaz-Arango, Héctor Vázquez-Leal, Francisco Marroquín-Gutiérrez

Biology 2026, 15(12), 947 · Behavioural Biology section · Published 17 June 2026 · doi.org/10.3390/biology15120947

Read the article Download PDF

What the study is about

Insects constantly adjust how they move according to their surroundings, and temperature and light are two of the strongest cues. Understanding that relationship is key both for neuroethology and for the development of insect-based biorobots, which is the broader goal of the Cyborg Insects Initiative.

In this work we built a low-cost, controlled-environment platform: a spherical treadmill with optical motion tracking inside a chamber where temperature and illumination are programmed and synchronized with the recordings, while a thermal camera monitors the animal. With it, we measured the tethered walking of Madagascar hissing cockroaches (Gromphadorhina portentosa) at six temperatures between 18 and 38 °C, and ran exploratory assays with UV and green light.

The locomotion signal was reconstructed from two orthogonal optical sensors and described with metrics of activity intensity, continuity and path organization, which were then analyzed with mixed-effects models, Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests and Spearman correlations.

Key findings

🌡️

A sweet spot near 30 °C

Activity measures such as cumulative activity, peak activity rate and peak modulation tended to rise up to about 30 °C, pointing to a non-linear relationship between temperature and locomotion.

🔥

Heat fragments movement

At 34 and 38 °C the cockroaches kept their peak activity but spent more time inactive, so their walking became more fragmented instead of sustained.

💡

UV light stands out

In the exploratory illumination assays, UV light was linked to the largest increases in activity and the clearest drop in inactivity; green light had only slight effects. These results are preliminary and need larger samples.

⚖️

Not explained by size or sex

Neither body length nor sex accounted for the main thermal pattern, supporting temperature itself as the driver of the observed changes.

Inside the study

Why it matters

The results provide a quantitative framework for studying how the environment modulates locomotion in G. portentosa, and the platform itself is an accessible, reproducible tool for future behavioural and bio-inspired research — including the design of biohybrid robots that need to perform reliably under changing conditions. This work grew out of my professional practices and social service at INAOE, and was selected by the journal as an Editor’s Choice article.

Initiative

Cyborg Insects Initiative (C2I)

Insects are renowned for their superior biomechanical capabilities compared to their robotic counterparts, which are often inefficient in chaotic environments and are more costly and challenging to produce. The Cyborg Insects Initiative (C2I) is a project in which I am engaged, focusing on the development of efficient biobots inspired by insects.

To achieve this objective, current research primarily involves characterizing various insect species under different environmental conditions, as well as analyzing their physiological activity using specialized instruments. Please feel free to contact me if you are interested in contributing to this initiative or wish to learn more about this research field.

Current advances

01

Machine learning for improving cockroach locomotion

We are developing efficient machine learning algorithms for strategically stimulating the cyborg cockroaches, so they do not become habituated to the stimulus and their movement can be controlled efficiently.

02

QR detection for motion tracking using videogrammetry

We developed a videogrammetry method for tracking the cockroach’s motion using only a webcam and a QR code. This lets us analyze its movement without adding a heavy electronic backpack or using complex tracking systems.

03

Efficient stimulation

We have put a lot of effort into characterizing Gromphadorhina portentosa behaviour under different stimulation parameters, to find the signal types that give the most efficient movement control.
